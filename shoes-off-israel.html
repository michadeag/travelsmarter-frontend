Do You Take Your Shoes Off When Entering a Home in Israel?

Removing shoes when entering a home is not a widespread or expected norm in Israel — most Israeli households keep shoes on indoors. You generally won't be expected to remove them unless the host specifically asks.

Unlike parts of East Asia or some Middle Eastern countries, Israel does not have a strong cultural tradition of removing shoes at the door. That said, some families — particularly those with roots in certain communities or who simply prefer cleaner floors — do ask guests to remove shoes, so follow the host's lead or look for a pile of shoes near the entrance as a signal. Religious observance level, family background, and personal preference vary considerably from household to household. This norm does not meaningfully extend to other indoor spaces like restaurants, offices, or shops.

💡 Glance at the entryway when you arrive — if you see shoes lined up near the door, quietly remove yours; otherwise, keep them on unless told otherwise.

Frequently asked questions

Are there any types of Israeli homes where shoe removal is more likely to be expected?

Some families with origins in certain Asian or North African Jewish communities may be more accustomed to removing shoes at the door, though even this is far from universal. Very observant households may have specific practices around cleanliness, but there's no single religious rule that makes shoe removal standard. Your safest cue is always what you observe at the entrance.

Do I need to remove my shoes in synagogues or other religious spaces in Israel?

In most synagogues, shoes stay on — there's no general Jewish religious requirement to remove footwear before entering. The exception is on Yom Kippur, when many observant Jews wear non-leather shoes or go without shoes as part of the fast day's customs. Outside of that specific occasion, you won't be expected to remove shoes in Jewish religious settings.

What about Airbnbs or guesthouses in Israel — should I ask about shoes?

For Airbnbs and private guesthouses, there's no standard expectation either way in Israel. If the host has a preference, they'll usually mention it in their welcome message or at check-in. When in doubt, a quick ask — 'Do you prefer shoes off?' — is perfectly polite and will be understood without any awkwardness.
