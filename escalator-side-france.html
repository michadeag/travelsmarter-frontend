Which Side Do You Stand On an Escalator in France?

Paris and other French cities loosely follow the 'stand right, walk left' convention on escalators, particularly in busy Métro and RER stations, but it is not strictly observed and enforcement is informal at best.

The stand-right convention exists as a social norm in Paris's transit network, and most commuters in busy stations like Châtelet-Les Halles or Gare du Nord will gravitate toward it during rush hour. However, outside peak times, in smaller cities, or among tourists, the convention is frequently ignored without any social friction. Paris's RATP transit authority has actually experimented with asking passengers to stand on both sides during certain trials, arguing that 'walk left' lanes reduce overall escalator capacity. So the norm is real but soft — don't expect it to be policed or universally followed.

💡 In a busy Paris Métro station during rush hour, default to standing on the right side of the escalator; if you want to walk up, move to the left and be prepared to keep a brisk pace.

Frequently asked questions

Will Parisians say something if I stand on the left side of an escalator?

During busy commuting hours in a major station, you may get a pointed look or a quiet 'excusez-moi' if you are blocking the left side. Outside rush hour the reaction is generally indifferent. A polite step to the right resolves any tension immediately.

Is the convention the same on escalators in cities outside Paris, like Lyon or Marseille?

The stand-right norm is weakest outside Paris — in Lyon, Marseille, and other French cities it exists even more loosely, if at all. Transit networks in those cities are less intensely used, so informal queuing norms around escalators are less established.

Does the same rule apply to moving walkways in French airports and train stations?

Moving walkways, such as those at Charles de Gaulle Airport or in large mainline train stations, broadly follow the same informal stand-right convention, though again it is inconsistently observed. Keeping right is a reasonable default that won't cause confusion anywhere in France.
