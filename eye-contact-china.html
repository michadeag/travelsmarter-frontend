Is Direct Eye Contact Polite or Rude in China?

Steady but moderate eye contact is generally fine in China, but prolonged, unblinking eye contact with elders or authority figures can feel confrontational rather than respectful.

In everyday conversations among peers, normal eye contact is expected and not considered unusual. However, traditional Confucian values around hierarchy mean that holding an intense, fixed gaze at someone older or of higher status can read as challenging or disrespectful — a brief, natural glance away is often the more deferential choice. Younger, urban Chinese people tend to follow similar norms to what you'd find in most Western contexts, so the hierarchy-related nuance matters more in formal or intergenerational settings than in casual city life. This is not a hard universal rule, and many people will simply not think much about a foreigner's eye contact habits.

💡 In meetings with senior officials, elders, or formal hosts, let your gaze be engaged but relaxed — glance away occasionally rather than holding eye contact the entire time, which signals respect without seeming evasive.

Frequently asked questions

Will Chinese people be offended if I maintain normal Western-style eye contact?

Most likely not, especially in urban areas and with people accustomed to international visitors. Chinese people generally don't expect foreigners to follow every local social nuance perfectly. That said, softening your gaze slightly in formal or hierarchical situations is a small, easy adjustment that signals cultural awareness.

Does avoiding eye contact come across as dishonest or shifty in China?

Not in the same way it might in some Western cultures. Looking down or away briefly in the presence of an elder or superior is traditionally a sign of modesty and respect, not deception. Complete avoidance of any eye contact in a peer-to-peer conversation, however, could feel awkward or disengaged.

Are there specific settings — like business meetings — where I should be especially mindful of this?

Yes, formal business meetings — particularly with senior executives or government officials — are the setting where the hierarchy-related norms are most relevant. A respectful, engaged expression matters more than the precise amount of eye contact; being attentive and nodding appropriately generally reads better than worrying too much about exactly where to look.
