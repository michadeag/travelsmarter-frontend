Is It Expected to Give Up Your Seat on Public Transport in Sri Lanka?

Offering your seat to elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers is a genuine social expectation in Sri Lanka, particularly on buses, though enforcement is informal and inconsistent rather than strict.

Sri Lankan buses do have designated priority seats — typically marked near the front — and the official expectation is that they go to elderly, pregnant, disabled, and sometimes clergy passengers. In practice, whether these seats are actually vacated depends heavily on the individual, how crowded the vehicle is, and the setting (urban Colombo buses versus rural routes can feel quite different). On trains, the norm is similar but slightly more relaxed, and designated sections are less consistently enforced. Younger Sri Lankans in cities may be less reliably observant of this custom than older generations, though offering your seat will always be appreciated.

💡 If you're a visitor and you see an elderly or pregnant person standing near priority seats that are occupied, feel free to gesture and offer your own seat — it's a simple act that will be warmly received and sets a good example regardless of what locals around you are doing.

Frequently asked questions

Are priority seats on Sri Lankan buses actually marked or labeled?

Yes, most public buses have seats near the front designated for elderly, pregnant, disabled, and sometimes Buddhist monks or clergy. These are often labeled in Sinhala, Tamil, or with symbols, but the signage quality varies by bus age and route. Don't count on clear English labeling.

Will I be verbally called out if I sit in a priority seat and don't give it up?

Overt public confrontation is uncommon — Sri Lankan social culture generally favors indirect communication over direct scolding of strangers. You're more likely to get a pointed look or quiet expectation than a loud reprimand, but giving up the seat when someone who needs it is standing is clearly the expected behavior.

Is this expectation the same on trains as on buses?

Trains have some reserved sections (such as for women and for disabled passengers in certain carriages), but the informal seat-offering norm is somewhat less rigidly observed than on buses. Intercity trains with reserved seating have their own ticketing rules, so 'giving up your seat' mainly applies to unreserved carriages during busy travel.
