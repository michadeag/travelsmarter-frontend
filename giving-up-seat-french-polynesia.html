Is It Expected to Give Up Your Seat on Public Transport in French Polynesia?

Offering your seat to elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers is a normal social courtesy in French Polynesia, as it is in most places, but it is not an unusually strong or enforced cultural norm. Designated priority seats exist on some public transport, particularly le truck (the traditional open-air bus) and newer buses in Papeete, though compliance is informal rather than rigidly policed.

French Polynesia's public transport network is modest — Tahiti has the most developed system, centered on le truck and standard buses along the coastal road. Priority seating markings, where they exist, follow the same general convention seen globally (seats near the front reserved for elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers), but enforcement is social rather than official. The culture is generally relaxed and polite; most locals would offer a seat without being asked, but there is no notable peer pressure or cultural sanction beyond ordinary politeness. On smaller islands, formal public transport is minimal or nonexistent, making the question largely irrelevant outside Tahiti.

💡 If you're unsure, simply standing and gesturing toward your seat is universally understood and appreciated — no French or Tahitian phrase required.

Frequently asked questions

Are priority seats clearly marked on buses in Tahiti?

On newer buses and some le truck routes in the Papeete area, you may see priority seat markings near the front, similar to what you'd find in France or other countries. However, the signage is inconsistent across vehicles, and older le trucks often have no markings at all. Don't rely on signage — just use common sense about who might need a seat more than you.

Is public transport in French Polynesia even common enough for this to matter?

On Tahiti, le truck and buses are a genuine mode of daily transport for locals, so the etiquette does apply in practice. On other islands like Moorea or Bora Bora, most visitors and many residents get around by rental car, scooter, or bicycle, and formal public buses are rare or non-existent. Outside Tahiti, the situation is unlikely to come up.

Will locals react negatively if I don't give up my seat?

A strong negative social reaction is unlikely — the atmosphere on local transport is generally laid-back. That said, remaining seated while an elderly or visibly pregnant person stands would be noticed and considered inconsiderate, just as it would anywhere. Erring on the side of offering your seat is always the safer and more respectful choice.
