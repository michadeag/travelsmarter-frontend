Is It Expected to Give Up Your Seat on Public Transport in French Polynesia?

Offering your seat to elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers is a basic courtesy expected in French Polynesia, but it is not enforced with unusual strictness — this follows the general norm found in most countries.

Public transport in French Polynesia is limited primarily to the island of Tahiti, where buses (le truck and modern coaches) and a small ferry network serve most passengers. Designated priority seats exist on newer buses and are marked, similar to standard practice elsewhere, but enforcement is informal and social rather than legal. The culture is generally relaxed and community-oriented, so offering a seat is seen as polite and respectful, but you are unlikely to face strong public shaming if you miss a cue. Locals and visitors alike are generally expected to read the situation and act with common sense.

💡 If you see a marked priority seat (often colored or labeled) and a person who clearly needs it boards, simply stand and gesture — a small smile goes a long way in Polynesian social culture.

Frequently asked questions

Are priority seats officially designated on buses in Tahiti?

On newer public buses in Tahiti, priority seats are typically marked near the front of the vehicle, similar to buses in France and other countries. Older 'le truck' style buses may not have formal markings, so common sense applies. There is no strict legal enforcement, but occupying a marked seat while someone who needs it is standing would be considered poor manners.

Is public transport in French Polynesia common enough that this situation often comes up?

Public transport is used regularly in Papeete and surrounding areas of Tahiti, so yes, crowded buses are a realistic scenario, especially during morning and evening rush hours. On outer islands, formal public transport is scarce or nonexistent, making this largely a Tahiti-specific consideration. Most visitors to resort islands like Bora Bora or Moorea will rely on hotel shuttles or taxis instead.

Is there anything culturally specific about Polynesian etiquette I should know when giving up a seat?

Polynesian culture generally values warmth, respect for elders ('taote' and respect for the elderly is embedded in fa'a'ā culture), and a relaxed, non-confrontational manner. Offering your seat quietly with a nod or smile is the right approach — making a loud gesture of it is unnecessary. Respecting older passengers in particular aligns well with local values and will be genuinely appreciated.
