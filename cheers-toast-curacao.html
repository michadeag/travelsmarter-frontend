How Do You Say "Cheers" in Curaçao, and Do You Clink Glasses?

The most common toast in Curaçao is 'Salú' (from Papiamentu, meaning 'health'), and clinking glasses is standard, friendly practice there.

Papiamentu is the local creole language of Curaçao, and 'Salú' is the everyday toast you'll hear in bars, at family gatherings, and at restaurants across the island. Dutch speakers may say 'Proost' instead, and Spanish-influenced 'Salud' is also understood, reflecting the island's multilingual character. Clinking glasses is the norm in casual and social settings, and maintaining eye contact while clinking is considered polite — skipping eye contact is sometimes jokingly said to bring bad luck, a belief shared with several European traditions that Dutch colonialism helped spread.

💡 Just say 'Salú' and meet eyes with each person as you clink — locals will appreciate the effort and it fits naturally in any setting from a beach bar to a family dinner.

Frequently asked questions

Is 'Salú' understood everywhere in Curaçao, or only in certain settings?

'Salú' works universally across Curaçao since Papiamentu is spoken by the vast majority of the local population. Even in more formal or tourist-oriented venues, using 'Salú' reads as respectful and locally aware rather than out of place.

Do people in Curaçao really believe skipping eye contact while clinking brings bad luck?

It's a widely held superstition on the island, likely inherited from Dutch and broader European drinking culture, but it's taken lightly — more of a playful social norm than a serious belief. You won't cause offense by forgetting, but locals may teasingly remind you.

Are there any drinks particularly associated with toasting in Curaçao?

Blue Curaçao liqueur is the island's most famous export, but locals are just as likely to toast with local Amstel Bright beer or rum-based cocktails. There's no single ceremonial drink tied to toasting — the toast itself matters more than what's in the glass.
