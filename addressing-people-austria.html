First Names or Titles? How to Address People in Austria

In Austria, formal titles and surnames are the default with new acquaintances and colleagues — wait until you are explicitly invited to use first names before switching.

Austria has a notably stronger culture of formal address than many neighboring countries, including Germany, and especially compared to English-speaking norms. In professional settings, Herr/Frau plus surname (or professional title like Doktor or Magister) is standard until one party suggests moving to first names, often phrased as 'Wir können uns duzen.' Academic and professional titles are taken seriously and omitting them can come across as slightly dismissive, particularly with older Austrians or in traditional sectors like law, medicine, and government. Younger Austrians and startup or tech environments tend to be more relaxed about this, so context matters.

💡 When in doubt, mirror whoever you're speaking with — use their title and surname until they offer their first name or explicitly suggest 'du' (the informal 'you'), then match their lead.

Frequently asked questions

What titles should I use when addressing Austrians formally?

Use Herr (Mr.) or Frau (Mrs./Ms.) followed by the surname as a baseline. If someone holds an academic or professional title — Doktor, Magister, Professor — it's polite to use it, e.g., 'Herr Doktor Müller.' Austrians often include these titles on business cards, which gives you a useful cue.

Is formal address expected in every situation, or just in the workplace?

It's most important in professional, academic, and official contexts, as well as when meeting someone significantly older than you. In casual social settings — a bar, a young friend group, or informal tourism contexts — first names and 'du' are perfectly normal from the start. Read the setting and follow the other person's tone.

What happens if I accidentally use someone's first name too soon?

Most Austrians, especially those accustomed to international visitors, won't be offended — they'll understand it's a cultural difference. That said, erring on the formal side is always the safer choice in professional or formal situations, as it shows respect rather than familiarity. A quick apology and correction if you notice someone's discomfort is perfectly acceptable.
